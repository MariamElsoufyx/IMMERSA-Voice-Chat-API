The building styles of the Royal School of Engineering (the Faculty of Engineering from 1935), occupying its purpose-built campus completed in 1905 at Giza bordering the Giza Zoo and facing Orman Park, reflected the dominant institutional architecture of Khedival and early-monarchy Egypt. This was an eclectic blend characteristic of the era: European Beaux-Arts and neoclassical planning principles (symmetrical facades, central axes, monumental entrances, ordered rows of tall windows) combined with Islamic Revival and Neo-Mamluk decorative elements such as pointed and horseshoe arches, carved stone detailing, mashrabiya-influenced screening, and occasional domes or crenellated parapets. Walls were typically of masonry and brick with stone or rendered facades, often in pale stone or ochre tones suited to the Egyptian light.

Buildings were generally low-rise, two to three storeys, with thick walls and high ceilings designed for the hot climate, allowing air circulation and shade. Large workshop and laboratory halls had taller volumes, industrial roof structures, and big windows or clerestory glazing to admit daylight for precise drafting and machining. As the campus expanded through the 1920s and 1930s, and especially after the broader Egyptian University consolidated its faculties at Giza from 1929, newer buildings tended toward cleaner, more functional lines with less ornament, reflecting the international shift toward modern, utilitarian institutional architecture, while the original 1905 core retained its more decorative revivalist character. Note: precise architectural records (architect names, exact ornamentation) for the engineering school's specific buildings are sparse in readily available sources; this description reconstructs the style from the documented Giza setting and the well-attested architectural conventions of Egyptian educational buildings of the period, and is intended for worldbuilding rather than as a verified blueprint.
