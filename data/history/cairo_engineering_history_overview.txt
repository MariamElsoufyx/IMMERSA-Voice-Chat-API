The Faculty of Engineering at Cairo University is the most prestigious engineering faculty in Egypt and is widely regarded as the first engineering school established in the Middle East and Africa. Its history dates back to 1816, when Muhammad Ali Pasha ordered the establishment of the Muhandiskhana (School of Engineering), which was originally located at the Citadel of Salah al-Din al-Ayyubi in Cairo. It was relocated several times before settling at its current site in Giza in 1905, and was incorporated into Cairo University as one of its faculties in 1935.

The Mohandeskhana, whose name comes from a Turkish-Arabic compound meaning "House of the Engineer," was established in Cairo in 1816 under Muhammad Ali Pasha as part of his broad program to modernize Egypt. It was one of the earliest engineering schools in the region and was created to train engineers for the state's growing infrastructure, industrial, and military needs. The first school was modelled on the French École d'Ingénieurs and drew its curriculum and pedagogical methods from the French engineering tradition, which was considered the most advanced in Europe at the time.

The beginning of engineering education in Egypt traces back to when Muhammad Ali Pasha established the Muhandiskhana at the Citadel in 1816 in the form of preliminary classes. The school was headed in its early years by Hassan Afandy, known as El-Darwish El-Mousely, who oversaw its initial development. It officially opened its doors as a regular institution in Bulaq, a district of Cairo, in 1834, but was closed in September 1854 under the reign of Sa'id Pasha. Subsequently, in December 1858, two schools were established: one for irrigation engineering at the Qanater Khairiya (Delta Barrages) and another for architecture and building at the Citadel. Both of these institutions were closed again in 1861.

In its early decades the school taught mathematics, surveying, fortification, civil and military engineering, and mechanical drawing. Instruction drew heavily on European technical traditions, and several of its early instructors were brought in from France and Italy to help establish the curriculum. Muhammad Ali Pasha also sent educational missions of Egyptian students to France and Italy beginning in the 1820s, where they studied engineering, medicine, and military science. These returning students later joined the teaching staff of the Muhandiskhana and other state schools, and translators such as Rifa'a al-Tahtawi rendered key French scientific and technical works into Arabic to support local instruction.

Later, a comprehensive school for irrigation and architecture was reopened by Khedive Ismail (son of Muhammad Ali Pasha) in 1866, temporarily housed at Zaafaran Palace in Abbasiya. The following year, in 1867, it was transferred to the palace of Mustafa Fadel Pasha in the Darb al-Gamamiz district of Cairo. The school consisted of two departments: one for irrigation and another for architecture. The duration of study was five years, including one preparatory year, with specialization taking place during the final two years. The preparatory year was later abolished, reducing the study period to four years, while specialization remained in the last two years.

The specialization system was subsequently abolished, and the school was converted into a single department. The school was also temporarily relocated to the premises of the old School of Agriculture in Giza, pending the completion of its new buildings at the current site in 1905, at which point it was renamed the Royal School of Engineering (Madrasat al-Handasa al-Malakiyya). The new Giza campus, designed in a neo-classical style typical of late-Khedival public architecture, included specialized laboratories, drafting halls, and workshops that allowed the school to expand the practical side of its curriculum.

The institution moved locations and changed names several times during the nineteenth century as Egypt's educational system was reorganized under successive rulers, including Sa'id Pasha and Khedive Ismail. Despite these disruptions, it remained the country's principal centre for formal engineering education and produced many of the engineers who worked on irrigation networks, railways, bridges, ports, and public works projects across Egypt, including major undertakings on the Nile barrages and the early phases of the Egyptian railway system, which was itself the first railway built in Africa and the Middle East.

After settling in Giza, the school was once again divided into two departments for irrigation and architecture, while maintaining the previous system of study. In 1916, the school was reorganized into five departments: Irrigation, Architecture, Municipal Engineering, Mechanics, and Electricity. In 1926, these departments were restructured into four main divisions: Civil Engineering, Architectural Engineering, Mechanical Engineering, and Electrical Engineering. These departments later branched out into various sub-disciplines, accompanied by the establishment and expansion of laboratories, lecture halls, and research facilities to keep pace with developments in modern engineering science.

In 1935, the Royal School of Engineering was annexed to the Egyptian University, which had been founded as a private institution on 21 December 1908 and was later taken over by the state in 1925. The university was renamed Fuad I University in 1940 and finally became Cairo University in 1954 following the Egyptian Revolution of 1952. Upon its incorporation, the Royal School of Engineering became known as the Faculty of Engineering, and new departments were progressively established within it to cover emerging fields such as chemical, aerospace, computer, communications, and biomedical engineering.

The faculty followed a traditional two-semester academic system, with annual end-of-year examinations determining progression, until it formally adopted the credit hours system in 2006, bringing its structure closer to international academic standards. Since its founding in 1816, the Faculty of Engineering has been led by more than 35 deans, each of whom contributed to the development of its educational programs, research output, and links with industry and society. Today, the faculty is administered by a dean assisted by three vice-deans responsible for academic and student affairs, postgraduate studies and research, and community service and environmental affairs, and it remains one of the most highly ranked engineering schools in Egypt and the Arab world.z
