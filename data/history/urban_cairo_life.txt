Cairo between 1910 and 1935 was a city of vivid contrasts, simultaneously ancient and rapidly modernizing, and a cosmopolitan hub at the center of the Arab world. The city was effectively divided between the older quarters, dense, historic neighborhoods of narrow lanes, mosques, markets (suqs), and traditional courtyard houses around Islamic Cairo and al-Azhar, and the newer European-style downtown (Ismailiyya/Ezbekiyya and beyond) laid out with broad boulevards, squares, apartment blocks, department stores, hotels, banks, and grand public buildings in Beaux-Arts and Neo-Mamluk styles. Affluent new suburbs such as Garden City, Zamalek, Maadi, and Heliopolis developed for the wealthy and foreign communities.

Daily life mixed the modern and the traditional: trams clanged through the streets, automobiles and horse-drawn carriages shared the roads with donkeys and carts, street vendors and water-sellers called their wares, and the calls to prayer punctuated the day. The downtown was alive with cafes and coffeehouses (a vital social institution where men gathered to talk, read newspapers, smoke shisha, and discuss politics), cinemas, theaters, cabarets, and nightclubs, especially around Ezbekiyya, giving Cairo a famous nightlife and entertainment scene. A large foreign population, many languages on the streets (Arabic, French, English, Greek, Italian), and a thriving press made the city feel international. Yet great poverty coexisted with this glamour, with crowded popular quarters and a struggling working class living close to the wealth of the elite and foreigners.
