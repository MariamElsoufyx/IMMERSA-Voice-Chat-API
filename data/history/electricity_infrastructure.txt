Electricity infrastructure in Egypt between 1915 and 1935 was limited, localized, and concentrated in cities, with no national grid. Power was generated and distributed by private and municipal concession companies, principally serving Cairo, Alexandria, and other towns, rather than by a unified state authority (the state electricity authorities and a national grid emerged only decades later, from the 1960s-1970s onward). Generation was overwhelmingly thermal, using steam plants and reciprocating engines or steam turbines burning fuel oil, since Egypt had no domestic coal and large-scale Nile hydroelectricity had not yet been developed. Electricity powered urban lighting, electric tramways (Cairo's tram network began in 1896 on 600 V DC overhead supply), the Cairo-Helwan electric railway (opened 1902), some pumping, and a growing number of factories.

Electrical engineering was a recognized and taught specialization, established as a distinct department at the engineering school from the 1916 reorganization. The technology of the period used alternating-current and direct-current systems, rotating machines (generators and motors), transformers, switchgear, overhead lines, and electromechanical metering and measuring instruments. However, electrification was far from universal: rural Egypt was largely without electricity, and supply was uneven even in cities. Crucially, hydroelectric power from the Aswan Dam (the Aswan hydroelectric station was added much later, around 1960, and the High Dam in 1970), a nationally interconnected transmission grid, nuclear power, and solid-state/electronic power control did NOT exist in Egypt during 1915-1935. Power systems were small, isolated, and based on early-twentieth-century rotating-machinery and thermal-generation technology.
