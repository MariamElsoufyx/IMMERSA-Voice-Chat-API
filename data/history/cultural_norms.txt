Cultural norms in Egypt between 1910 and 1935 were anchored in religion, family, honor, and hierarchy, while absorbing growing Western influence among the urban classes. Islam shaped the rhythm of daily life for the Muslim majority (prayer times, Ramadan, religious festivals), and the Coptic Christian minority observed its own calendar; relations between the two communities were generally framed by the nationalist ideal of unity. The extended family was the central social unit, with strong expectations of respect for elders, deference to fathers and male authority, and family honor closely tied to the conduct and seclusion of women, especially in the upper classes.

Dress signaled identity and status: the tarboosh (fez) with a Western suit became the emblem of the educated effendi middle class, while traditional gallabiyas were worn by the popular classes and peasants, and elite women's veiling marked their rank. Forms of address and titles (Pasha, Bey, Effendi) were socially important. Hospitality, generosity, and elaborate courtesy were prized values. Coffeehouse sociability (for men), conversation, poetry, music, and storytelling were central to public culture. At the same time, Western manners, fashions, consumer goods, cinema, and entertainments spread among the wealthy and middle classes, sometimes creating friction between modernizing aspirations and conservative or religious sensibilities, a tension that ran through debates over women's roles, education, and the proper balance between authentic Egyptian/Islamic identity and European modernity.
