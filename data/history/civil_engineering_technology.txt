Civil engineering technology in Egypt between 1915 and 1935 centered overwhelmingly on water control, public works, and transport infrastructure rather than on tall buildings or heavy private industry. The dominant works were Nile barrages and dams, irrigation canals and drainage networks, bridges, roads, and railway permanent way, mostly designed and supervised under the British-led Ministry of Public Works. Masonry, brickwork, cut stone (including Aswan granite for major hydraulic works), and increasingly reinforced concrete and structural steel were the principal structural materials. The era saw the Aswan Low Dam heightened a second time between 1929 and 1933 to raise its storage capacity, and the completion of the Nag Hammadi Barrage in 1930, both major civil engineering undertakings of the period.

Civil engineering was practiced and taught with a strong hydraulics and irrigation emphasis, reflecting Egypt's agrarian, cotton-export economy. Reinforced concrete (using Portland cement, steel reinforcing bars, and timber formwork) was an established and growing technology, used in bridges, barrage works, and buildings, and was taught as a subject at the engineering school. Structural analysis relied on hand calculation using slide rules, logarithm tables, and graphical statics; there were no electronic computers, no finite-element analysis, and no computer-aided design. Survey-based earthwork, gravity-fed canal design, and empirical hydraulic formulae were standard. Steel-framed and reinforced-concrete construction existed, but the high-rise tower and prestressed concrete (which became widespread internationally only after the late 1930s and 1940s) were essentially absent from Egyptian practice in this period.
