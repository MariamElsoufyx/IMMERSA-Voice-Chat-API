
Relations were formal and strongly hierarchical. Professors — a mix of British engineers and a growing cadre of Egyptians, some trained on government missions to Europe — commanded considerable deference. Students addressed them with elaborate respect and rarely challenged them openly in the lecture hall. Yet the political climate strained these bonds: nationalist students could resent British instructors as agents of occupation, and demands for Egyptianization of the staff grew louder after 1922. The relationship blended genuine reverence for technical mastery with underlying friction over authority, nationality, and the colonial order.
