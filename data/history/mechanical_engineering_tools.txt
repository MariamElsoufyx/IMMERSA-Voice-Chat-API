Mechanical engineering tools and machines in Egypt between 1915 and 1935 were dominated by steam power, line-shaft-driven workshops, and imported European machinery, reflecting an economy that was industrializing slowly from a primarily agricultural base. Steam engines and steam turbines were the principal prime movers for railways, pumping, ginning, and power generation, while internal-combustion engines (petrol and diesel) were present and growing for pumps, road vehicles, and small plant. Workshops, including those built at the Royal School of Engineering for teaching, contained machine tools such as lathes, drilling machines, shaping and planing machines, milling machines, grinders, and steam/boiler apparatus, typically driven by belts from overhead line shafts powered by a central steam or electric engine.

Mechanical engineering was an established taught specialization (a distinct department from the 1916 reorganization, later combined with electrical as the Mechanical-Electrical/Power department in 1925-1926). Core technologies included thermodynamics and steam-plant practice, machine design, gearing and power transmission, pumps, and the testing of materials and machines. Measuring and workshop instruments included micrometers, calipers, gauges, indicators, and dynamometers. Importantly, numerically controlled (CNC) machine tools, computer-aided manufacturing, robotics, hydraulics-and-electronics automation, and gas-turbine and jet propulsion did NOT exist in Egyptian practice in this period; machining was manual and operator-skill-dependent, and the most advanced widely used technologies were the steam turbine and the diesel engine. Cotton ginning and textile machinery, sugar-mill machinery, and pumping plant were among the most common industrial mechanical installations.
