Textbooks and references used at the engineering school during 1910-1940 were predominantly European, in keeping with the school's foreign-modeled instruction and European-language teaching. During the period of strong British control of the directorship and the Ministry of Public Works (the directors from 1902 to 1924 were British), English-language engineering textbooks and technical references dominated, supporting the practice-oriented, British-influenced curriculum. French texts retained some presence as a legacy of the earlier French-modeled school, and after the late-1920s appointment of the Swiss director Charles Andreae and the broader turn toward continental European models under King Fuad, German and continental European references and approaches also informed the upgraded syllabi.

Because Arabic had not yet replaced European languages as the medium of advanced technical instruction before 1940, students relied heavily on imported European textbooks, and competence in English or French was effectively a prerequisite for study. Subjects such as mathematics, mechanics, hydraulics, machine design, and electrical engineering were taught from standard early-twentieth-century European engineering literature of the kind used in British and continental schools. Note: specific, verified titles and authors of the exact textbooks prescribed at the Royal School of Engineering in this period are not well documented in readily available secondary sources; this entry therefore characterizes the general language and origin of the reference materials (predominantly English, with French and continental European works) rather than listing confirmed individual textbooks, to preserve historical accuracy.
