Modernization and Western influence were central, contested themes in Egyptian life between 1910 and 1935. Decades of European, especially British and French, presence had reshaped Cairo's downtown, education, professions, dress, consumer habits, and institutions. The wealthy and the rising effendi middle class adopted European suits, furniture, schooling, manners, and entertainments; department stores, banks, cinemas, cars, electricity, telephones, and the press marked a visibly modernizing urban world. European languages (French as the language of elite refinement, English through British administration) carried prestige, and study abroad in Europe was a path to status.

Yet modernization was double-edged and politically charged. Because so much of it arrived through colonial domination and benefited foreigners and the elite, Egyptians debated how to become modern without becoming subservient or losing their identity. This produced a spectrum of responses: liberal secularists who embraced European science and institutions while demanding national independence; economic nationalists like Talaat Harb who built Egyptian-owned industry (Bank Misr, textile mills) to reduce dependence on foreigners; Islamic modernists who sought to reconcile faith with progress; and, by the late 1920s and 1930s, more assertive movements (such as the Muslim Brotherhood, founded 1928, and the ultranationalist Young Egypt) reacting against Western cultural dominance. Engineering education itself embodied this tension, transmitting European technical knowledge and models (French, then British, then continental) to serve Egyptian development, while engineers and students increasingly framed their expertise as a tool of national self-strengthening and independence.
