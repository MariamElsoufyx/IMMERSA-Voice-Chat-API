Transportation in Egypt between 1910 and 1935 combined modern and traditional modes. Within Cairo, the electric tram was the backbone of public transit: the network had begun in 1896, ran on 600-volt overhead lines, and by this period was extensive, carrying crowds across the city and out to suburbs; tram workers were numerous enough that their strike helped paralyze Cairo during the 1919 Revolution. An electric suburban railway connected central Cairo to Helwan (from 1902), and electric interurban tram lines served Heliopolis. Automobiles and motor taxis were increasingly present but still a privilege of the well-off, sharing the streets with horse-drawn carriages (the elegant arabiya hantur), bicycles, donkeys, carts, and pedestrians.

Between cities, the Egyptian State Railways operated one of the oldest and densest networks in Africa and the Middle East, running standard-gauge steam trains (fueled by mazut, fuel oil) along the Nile Valley and across the Delta, with narrow-gauge light railways serving agricultural districts. The Nile itself remained a major artery, with feluccas, barges, and steamers carrying goods and passengers. River and rail connected Cairo to Alexandria, Upper Egypt, and the provinces. Air travel was just emerging at the very end of the period (Misr Air was founded in 1932). For most ordinary Egyptians, especially the poor and rural population, walking and animal transport remained the everyday reality, while trams, trains, and cars marked the modernizing urban experience of the middle and upper classes, including students commuting to schools and the university at Giza.
