The engineering workshops were among the most distinctive spaces on the campus, a direct legacy of the British emphasis on workshop training and apprenticeship and the school's need, given Egypt's thin industrial base, to provide practical training within its own premises. These were large, high-roofed industrial halls, often with skylights or clerestory windows and exposed roof trusses, floored in stone, concrete, or packed earth able to bear heavy machinery. Power was distributed by overhead line shafts and leather belts driven from a central steam or electric engine, so that rows of machine tools clattered and whirred together when the shafting turned.

Mechanical workshops held lathes, drilling machines, shaping and planing machines, milling machines, grinders, and benches fitted with vices and hand tools, where students learned turning, fitting, and machining. A forge or smithy with hearths, anvils, and the ring of hammered metal supported blacksmithing and metalwork, while a foundry area handled casting. Carpentry and pattern-making shops smelled of sawn timber and shellac. The air of the workshops carried the smells of hot metal, cutting oil, coal smoke, and machine grease, and the sounds of belts slapping, metal being cut, hammers striking, and instructors calling over the din. Students wore aprons or overalls and worked under the supervision of skilled foremen and demonstrators. Note: the existence and centrality of these workshops is well documented; the specific machine inventory and sensory detail here is reconstructed from standard early-twentieth-century engineering-workshop practice for worldbuilding, not from verified equipment lists of the Giza school.
