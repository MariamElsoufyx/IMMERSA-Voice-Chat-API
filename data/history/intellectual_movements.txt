Intellectual life in Egypt between 1910 and 1935 was dominated by the Nahda, the Arab "renaissance" or awakening, which reached a peak in this period with Cairo as its center. The Nahda combined revival of the Arabic language and heritage with engagement with European thought, science, and modernity, debating how Egypt should modernize while defining its own identity. A flourishing print culture, newspapers, magazines, and books, created a mass readership and a public sphere where ideas circulated rapidly; the press was a major political and cultural force.

Leading currents included liberal secular nationalism (associated with figures like Ahmed Lutfi el-Sayed, the "Professor of the Generation" and first rector of the Egyptian University, who promoted "Egypt for the Egyptians" and translated Aristotle), literary modernism and reform (the writer and educator Taha Hussein, whose 1926 book on pre-Islamic poetry caused great controversy and cost him his university post), and Islamic modernism descending from Muhammad Abduh, which sought to reconcile Islam with reason and modern life. Debates raged over the role of religion versus secularism, the status of women, the relationship of Egypt to Arab, Islamic, Mediterranean, and Pharaonic identities, and the merits of Western civilization. Salons (including women's salons such as May Ziadeh's), literary societies, and the new university fostered this ferment. The period also saw the birth of modern Egyptian theater, music, and cinema, and the rise of a self-consciously Egyptian national culture, making Cairo the intellectual and artistic capital of the Arabic-speaking world.
