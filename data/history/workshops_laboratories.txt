Practical workshops and laboratories were central to the engineering school's identity during 1910-1940, a direct legacy of British educational influence emphasizing workshop training and apprenticeship. Because Egypt lacked the developed industrial sector that allowed British engineering students to apprentice in outside factories and firms, the school was forced to build and operate its own internal workshops. This made the institution responsible for both the scientific and the practical formation of its students, an arrangement contemporaries criticized as a substitute for genuine industrial apprenticeship but accepted as necessary given local conditions.

The 1905 Giza buildings provided the physical setting for these facilities. The most significant expansion came under director Charles Andreae (1928-1937), formerly dean of the Zurich engineering school, who reorganized the curriculum and built a large number of new laboratories and workshops. These supported the mechanical and electrical departments in particular, providing machine shops, testing facilities, and electrical and measurement laboratories, alongside hydraulics and materials facilities serving the civil/irrigation and architecture tracks. Laboratory and workshop hours were embedded in the weekly schedule throughout the program, complementing lectures and exercise sessions. The hydraulics and irrigation laboratories were especially important given Egypt's dependence on Nile water management, supporting experiments in fluid mechanics, irrigation and drainage systems, and hydraulic structures. This in-house workshop-and-laboratory model, intensified by Andreae's investments, became a permanent characteristic of the institution as it transitioned into the Faculty of Engineering in 1935.
