Between 1910 and 1935, Egypt lived under the shadow of the British occupation that had begun in 1882. For the first part of this period Egypt was nominally an Ottoman province but effectively controlled by Britain through a Consul-General/High Commissioner; in December 1914, at the outbreak of World War I, Britain declared Egypt a formal protectorate and severed the Ottoman link, deposing the Khedive and installing a Sultan. The war years brought heavy burdens: requisitioning of crops and animals, the conscription of peasants into the Labour Corps, martial law, and censorship, which spread anti-British resentment from the educated elite to all classes.

After the 1919 Revolution, Britain issued a unilateral declaration of Egyptian independence on 28 February 1922, ending the protectorate and creating the Kingdom of Egypt under King Fuad I, with a constitution in 1923 and a parliament. But this independence was sharply limited: Britain reserved control over the security of imperial communications (the Suez Canal), the defence of Egypt, the protection of foreign interests and minorities, and the Sudan. British troops remained on Egyptian soil, British advisers stayed embedded in ministries, and the High Commissioner retained great influence. The result was a frustrating "veiled" or partial independence in which real sovereignty was contested. This unresolved situation, a king, a nationalist parliament, and the British all competing for power, defined Egyptian politics throughout the period and fed continuous nationalist agitation up to the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty.
