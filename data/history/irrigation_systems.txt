Irrigation systems were the most advanced and economically vital area of engineering technology in Egypt between 1915 and 1935, because the entire economy depended on Nile water for cotton and other crops. The core infrastructure was a system of barrages and dams that raised river levels and diverted water into large gravity-fed canals running parallel to the Nile, supplemented by drainage networks. The Old Aswan Dam (completed 1902) partially stored floodwater to permit perennial (year-round) irrigation and multiple crops; it was heightened in 1907-1912 and again in 1929-1933 to increase storage as cotton demand grew. The Delta Barrages, plus the Zifta (1901), Asyut (1902), Esna (1909), and Nag Hammadi (1930) barrages, regulated water along the river.

The transition from basin irrigation (which relied on the natural annual flood) to perennial irrigation was a defining technological feature of the period, enabling several harvests per year but also creating new engineering problems of drainage, waterlogging, and soil salinity that engineers worked to manage. British and British-trained engineers dominated the Irrigation Service, and Egypt's irrigation works were internationally significant: the related Sennar Dam and Gezira cotton scheme in Sudan, completed in 1925, were designed by Scottish engineer Sir Murdoch MacDonald and built by British contractors. Technology was hydraulic and gravity-based, using sluice gates, regulators, weirs, and open canals. Crucially, the Aswan High Dam, drip and sprinkler micro-irrigation, electric pumping at large scale, and computerized water management did NOT exist in this period; pumping where used relied on steam, diesel, and traditional devices such as the sakia (animal-powered waterwheel) and shaduf.
