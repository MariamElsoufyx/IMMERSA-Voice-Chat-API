Teaching methods at the Egyptian engineering school reflected the competing French and British educational traditions. The older French-influenced model privileged theoretical study, especially mathematics, in the manner of the Ecole Polytechnique and Ecole des Mines, gradually shifting toward the more industry-oriented Ecole Centrale model. After 1886, British administrators deliberately replaced French instructors with British ones who emphasized practice over theory, intending to prepare graduates who could immediately contribute useful engineering work, particularly to maximize cotton-crop output through irrigation works.

By the period 1910-1940, formal instruction combined lectures with exercise/problem sessions and hands-on laboratory and workshop training. The British emphasis on workshop training and apprenticeship was a defining feature, but Egypt's lack of an industrial base created a problem: unlike British students who could train in outside industrial firms, Egyptian students had few external sites for apprenticeship. The school was therefore compelled to provide practical engineering training within its own premises, building its own workshops, so that teaching had to cover both the scientific preparation of students and their introduction to practical engineering simultaneously. Because most graduates were destined directly for government posts, the pedagogy stressed producing engineers who could perform useful practical work immediately upon graduation. Under Charles Andreae's directorship (1928-1937), the methods were modernized with expanded laboratories and workshops and revised syllabi drawing on continental European (Swiss/German) practice, reinforcing the integration of theoretical lectures with supervised practical and laboratory work.
