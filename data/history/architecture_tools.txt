Architecture tools in Egypt between 1915 and 1935 were entirely manual drafting and drawing instruments, used by an architecture profession that was a long-established department at the engineering school (architecture had been one of the two original specializations of the nineteenth-century school). Drawing was produced by hand on paper or linen using drawing boards, T-squares, parallel rules, set squares, drafting compasses and dividers, French curves, protractors, and engineering/architectural scales. Drawings were inked with ruling pens and reproduced as blueprints (cyanotype) or, increasingly, as diazo (whiteprint) copies. Perspective drawing, watercolor and wash rendering, and physical scale models were the means of presenting designs. Lettering was done by hand or with stencils.

Architectural practice of the period combined European Beaux-Arts and revivalist styles with local and Islamic-influenced design, and made growing use of reinforced concrete and structural steel alongside traditional masonry, brick, and stone. The architecture curriculum emphasized drawing, building construction, materials, and the history of architecture. Importantly, the digital tools that define modern architectural practice did NOT exist in this period: there was no computer-aided design (CAD), no Building Information Modeling (BIM), no digital rendering or 3D visualization software, and no photocopying in the modern sense; all design documentation depended on hand drafting and chemical-process print reproduction. Structural calculations supporting architectural design were performed manually with slide rules and tables, and coordination between architect and engineer relied on physically drawn and printed sheets.
