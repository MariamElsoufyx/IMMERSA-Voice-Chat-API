Full name: Douglas Dunlop (Scottish teacher and missionary; active in Egypt c. 1888-1919). Field/specialization: education administration; British adviser to the Egyptian Ministry of Education (not an engineer). Historical importance: appointed by Lord Cromer as British "consultant"/adviser to the Egyptian Ministry of Education, Dunlop created what became known as the "Dunlop system," which shaped the language and staffing of government education, including higher technical and engineering instruction, during the British occupation.

Contributions (in the context of engineering education): Dunlop promoted English as the preferred medium of instruction, favored employing British teachers, and worked to marginalize teaching in Arabic and French in government schools. This reinforced the English-language, British-modeled environment in which the Royal School of Engineering operated in the 1900s and 1910s, meaning engineering students relied on English instruction and English-language textbooks. Teaching style/personality: widely seen as authoritarian and unsympathetic to Egyptian nationalist aspirations; notably, even after decades in Egypt he, like Cromer, did not speak Arabic. Influence on Egyptian engineering education: his policies entrenched European-language (especially English) technical instruction, a defining feature of the school before the nationalist push for Arabic. Political/social influence: Dunlop became a lightning rod for Egyptian nationalist resentment of British control over education; figures like Saad Zaghlul (Minister of Education from 1907) promoted Arabic against him, and Dunlop resigned around the 1919 Revolution, after which Egyptianization gradually advanced.
