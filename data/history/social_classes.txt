Egyptian society between 1910 and 1935 was steeply stratified. At the top sat a small landowning aristocracy and the royal court, many of Turco-Circassian origin, holding large estates and bearing titles such as Pasha and Bey; they dominated politics, owned much of the agricultural land, and lived in grand villas and palaces. Alongside them was a substantial foreign and minority community, Greeks, Italians, British, French, Jews, Armenians, Syrians, and others, who were prominent in commerce, banking, the professions, and cosmopolitan urban life, often protected by the Capitulations (legal privileges for foreigners).

The great majority were the fellahin (peasants) of the countryside, who farmed the land, mostly cotton, under landlords and lived in poverty with little education, and a growing urban working class of laborers, artisans, and the new factory workers of the emerging textile and processing industries. Between these poles a rising urban middle class was taking shape: the effendiyya, educated, salaried Egyptians, government clerks, teachers, lawyers, doctors, journalists, and engineers, who wore Western suits with the tarboosh (fez) and who became the backbone of nationalist politics and the readership of the press. University and professional-school students were drawn largely from this effendi class and from aspiring families investing in education as a route to status and government employment. Religious scholars (the ulama, centered on al-Azhar) formed another distinct status group. Social mobility was limited but increasingly possible through education, which made the schools, including the engineering school, sites of both ambition and political ferment.
