Surveying equipment in Egypt between 1915 and 1935 was optical-mechanical and highly developed for its time, because accurate surveying was essential to the country's vast irrigation, cadastral (land registration), and railway works. The standard instruments were the theodolite (for measuring horizontal and vertical angles), the dumpy or precise level with leveling staves (for determining heights and gradients, critical for gravity-fed canals), the plane table with alidade for topographic mapping, and chains, steel tapes, and measuring bands for distances. Compasses, clinometers, and aneroid barometers supported reconnaissance and height work. Egypt maintained an active Survey Department that produced detailed maps and ran the cadastral survey of agricultural land, work tied closely to taxation and irrigation management.

Surveying was a fundamental subject taught to civil and irrigation engineering students, combining field practice with computation. Calculations of areas, volumes (earthworks), and traverse adjustments were done by hand using logarithm tables, trigonometric tables, and slide rules. Triangulation networks established geodetic control across the country. Crucially, the technologies that define modern surveying did NOT exist in this period: there were no electronic distance measurement (EDM) instruments, no total stations, no GPS or satellite positioning, no laser levels, and no digital data logging or GIS. Aerial photography for mapping (photogrammetry) was emerging internationally in the 1920s-1930s and was beginning to be applied, but routine surveying in Egypt remained based on ground-based optical instruments, chains and tapes, and manual computation throughout 1915-1935.
