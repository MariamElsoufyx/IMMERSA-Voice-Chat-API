The administrative offices of the engineering school housed the directorship and the bureaucracy that ran a state-linked institution closely tied to the Ministries of Education and Public Works. At the head was the Director (variously titled headmaster or dean), a post held by British directors from 1902 to 1924 and then by continental Europeans such as the Swiss Louis Potterat and Charles Andreae (1928-1937), before the institution became a university faculty led by a dean after 1935. Around the director were offices for the secretary, registrar, clerks, accountants, and departmental heads, handling admissions, examinations, records, correspondence, staff, budgets, and the issuing of diplomas.

These offices typically occupied the more formal, decorated front portion of the main building, near the monumental entrance, with the director's office being the largest and best-appointed, furnished with a substantial desk, bookshelves, filing cabinets, and seating for visitors, and often hung with portraits, framed plans, or the royal insignia appropriate to a "Royal" school. The clerical rooms held ranks of desks, ledgers, typewriters, telephones (in the later part of the period), stamps, and stacks of paper files, with the dry smell of paper and ink and the clatter of typewriters and the murmur of administrative business. Note: the directorship sequence (British directors to 1924, Potterat, then Andreae 1928-1937) is documented; the specific descriptions of office layout, furnishing, and atmosphere are reconstructed from standard period institutional practice for worldbuilding rather than from verified records of the engineering school's offices.
