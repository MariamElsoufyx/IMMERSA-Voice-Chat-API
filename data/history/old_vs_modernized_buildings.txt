The contrast between the older and the modernized buildings on the engineering campus was a visible record of the institution's evolution between 1905 and the 1940s. The original core, the purpose-built 1905 buildings of the Royal School of Engineering at Giza, carried the eclectic Khedival-era character: heavier masonry, decorative Islamic Revival and Neo-Mamluk detailing combined with Beaux-Arts symmetry and ornament, thick walls, high ceilings, arcaded courtyards, and a generally low-rise, garden-set form suited to climate and to the era's taste for monumental, decorated institutional architecture.

As the school expanded, especially under the laboratory-and-workshop building program of director Charles Andreae (1928-1937) and after the institution joined the Egyptian University (which consolidated its faculties at Giza from 1929), newer structures tended toward plainer, more functional, and more utilitarian design: cleaner lines, less applied ornament, larger glazed openings and clerestories to flood laboratories and drawing studios with daylight, and roof and floor structures engineered for heavy machinery and modern equipment. The result was a campus of mixed periods, where ornate older blocks stood alongside more austere modern halls, and where the modern buildings prioritized lighting, ventilation for technical work, and the housing of expanding laboratory and workshop facilities over decorative expression. Note: the 1905 origin, the Andreae-era expansion, and the 1929 Giza consolidation are documented; the specific architectural contrasts described here are reconstructed from those facts and the general stylistic trajectory of Egyptian institutional architecture for worldbuilding, rather than from a verified building-by-building survey of the campus.
