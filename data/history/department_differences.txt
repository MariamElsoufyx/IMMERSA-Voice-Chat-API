The departments of the engineering school differed in subject matter, emphasis, prestige, and career destinations during 1910-1940. The Irrigation department (later folded into Civil engineering in the 1925-1926 reorganization) was the oldest, largest, and most prestigious track, reflecting Egypt's overwhelming economic dependence on Nile irrigation and the dominance of the Ministry of Public Works. It emphasized hydraulics, irrigation works and structures, water management, surveying, and large civil infrastructure, and its graduates filled the most numerous and influential government engineering posts. The Architecture department, also rooted in the school's nineteenth-century origins, focused on building design and construction, drawing, materials, and municipal/sanitary works, serving public buildings and urban infrastructure.

The Mechanical and Electrical departments were comparatively newer, introduced as distinct specializations in the 1916 reorganization, and were smaller because Egypt's limited industrial base offered fewer employment outlets than government irrigation and public-works service. Mechanical engineering covered machine design, steam and thermal engineering, and machine-shop practice; Electrical engineering covered electrical machines, measurements, and power generation and transmission. In the 1925-1926 restructuring, the five departments were consolidated into three: Civil, Architecture, and a combined Mechanical-Electrical (Power) department. All departments shared a common foundational core in mathematics, physics, mechanics, and drawing in the early/preparatory years before diverging into specialization in the final years. Across the board the civil and irrigation orientation carried the greatest weight in both enrollment and influence, while mechanical and electrical engineering, though growing, remained secondary in scale throughout the 1910-1940 period given the country's agrarian, infrastructure-focused economy.
