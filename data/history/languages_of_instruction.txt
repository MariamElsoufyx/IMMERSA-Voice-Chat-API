Language of instruction at the engineering school during 1910-1940 was shaped by Egypt's colonial situation and shifting foreign influence. Under the early French-modeled school of the nineteenth century, French had been prominent and theoretical, mathematics-heavy teaching followed French models such as the Ecole Polytechnique. After the British occupation began in 1882 and intensified under Lord Cromer, French instructors were progressively replaced by British ones, and English rose as a dominant language of higher technical instruction. The "Dunlop system," associated with Scottish education adviser Douglas Dunlop (influential until his resignation around the 1919 Revolution), promoted English-medium teaching and British teachers while marginalizing Arabic and French in government education.

Through the 1910s and 1920s, engineering instruction was therefore delivered largely in European languages, predominantly English given British control of the school's directorship (three British directors served from 1902 to 1924) and the Ministry of Public Works. Technical textbooks and references were overwhelmingly English (and to a lesser extent French), which required students to be functionally literate in a European language. Egyptian nationalist sentiment, strengthened by the 1919 Revolution and the 1922 declaration of nominal independence, pushed for greater use of Arabic and the "Egyptianization" of the faculty. From the late 1920s, with King Fuad reducing British influence and appointing the Swiss director Charles Andreae, the institution drew more on continental European models, but European-language instruction and references remained the norm throughout the 1910-1940 period. Arabic gained ground gradually but did not displace European-language technical teaching before 1940.
