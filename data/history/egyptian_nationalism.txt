Egyptian nationalism reached a defining peak in this period. Before World War I, nationalist agitation was largely confined to the educated elite, but wartime hardships spread anti-British feeling across all classes. The pivotal moment was the 1919 Revolution: when the British exiled the nationalist leader Saad Zaghlul and fellow members of the Wafd ("Delegation") in March 1919 after they sought to present Egypt's case for independence at the Paris Peace Conference, the country erupted in nationwide demonstrations, strikes, and unrest uniting students, workers, lawyers, civil servants, merchants, peasants, and both Muslims and Coptic Christians.

The Wafd Party, led by Zaghlul, became the dominant nationalist political force and won mass popular support, championing full independence and the unity of Egypt and Sudan. The revolution pushed Britain to grant nominal independence in 1922 and a constitution in 1923, under which the Wafd won elections and Zaghlul became prime minister in 1924. Through the late 1920s and early 1930s, politics remained a three-way struggle among the Wafd, the monarchy (King Fuad), and the British, with frequent constitutional crises, including the imposition of a more autocratic 1930 constitution. Nationalism flared again in the 1935-36 protests demanding restoration of the 1923 constitution, leading to a Wafd electoral victory and the 1936 Anglo-Egyptian Treaty. The symbolism of this nationalism, the crescent-and-cross emblem of Muslim-Christian unity, the figure of Zaghlul, and the slogan "Egypt for the Egyptians," pervaded public life.
