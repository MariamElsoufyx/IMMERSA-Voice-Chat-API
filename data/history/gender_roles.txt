Gender roles in Egypt between 1910 and 1935 were in visible tension between strong tradition and emerging change, varying greatly by class. Among the urban upper class, the harem system persisted into the early decades: elite women were secluded in separate household quarters and, when going out, wore the face veil; marriages were often arranged and to older men, sometimes when girls were very young. Peasant and working-class women, by contrast, worked in fields, markets, and workshops and were far less secluded, though bound by their own conservative norms. Across society, women's primary expected roles were as wives, mothers, and managers of the household.

This period saw the birth of the organized Egyptian women's movement. Women, including elite figures like Huda Sha'rawi, Safia Zaghlul, and Saiza Nabarawi, played a public, visible role in the 1919 nationalist demonstrations and boycotts. In 1923 Sha'rawi founded the Egyptian Feminist Union and famously removed her veil at a Cairo train station on returning from a conference in Rome, a symbolic act for elite women (the veil being mainly an upper-class practice). The movement campaigned for girls' education, a higher minimum marriage age, reform of personal-status and divorce laws, restriction of polygamy, and women's suffrage, though the 1923 electoral law denied women the vote and they were largely expected to retreat to domestic life after independence. Female education expanded, and the first women graduated from the Egyptian University in 1933, but higher technical fields like engineering remained overwhelmingly male throughout the period.
