Construction methods in Egypt between 1915 and 1935 combined traditional masonry craft with the increasing adoption of reinforced concrete and structural steel. Load-bearing construction in fired brick, cut stone, and rubble masonry with lime or cement mortar remained common for buildings, while major hydraulic works such as barrages and the Aswan Low Dam used massive masonry and cut granite. Reinforced concrete, using Portland cement, steel reinforcing bars, sand and aggregate, and timber formwork, was an established and growing technology used in bridges, water structures, floors, and framed buildings, and it was taught at the engineering school. Structural steel sections were imported and used for framing, roofs, and bridges.

Building and civil works were labor-intensive, relying heavily on manual labor with hand tools, supplemented by animal power and a limited number of machines. Concrete was typically mixed on site (by hand or with small mixers) and placed manually; lifting used timber scaffolding, derricks, block-and-tackle, and basic cranes; earthmoving for canals and earthworks used hand digging, baskets, and animal-hauled equipment, with steam-powered excavators and dredgers on the largest projects. Quality control and structural design depended on hand calculation, codes of practice imported from Britain and Europe, and experienced supervision. Crucially, modern construction technologies did NOT exist in this period: there were no tower cranes, no ready-mix concrete delivered by truck, no prestressed or precast-industrialized concrete systems in general use, no hydraulic excavators, and no power tools of the modern electric kind at scale. Construction was slow, manual, and craft-and-supervision intensive.
