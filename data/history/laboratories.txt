The laboratories of the engineering school were a defining feature of its campus, especially after the major expansion under Swiss director Charles Andreae (1928-1937), who built a large number of new laboratories and workshops. Because Egypt lacked an industrial base where students could train, the school had to provide practical and experimental facilities in-house. The laboratories were grouped by department and occupied tall, well-lit halls with large windows, sturdy benches, and the heavy fittings needed for testing equipment.

Hydraulics laboratories, the most important given Egypt's irrigation-centred engineering, contained flow channels (flumes), weirs, tanks, pumps, and measuring apparatus for studying fluid mechanics, water flow, and the behaviour of canals and hydraulic structures, often with the sound of running water and the smell of damp concrete and oiled metal. Materials-testing laboratories held large machines for testing the strength of concrete, steel, timber, and other construction materials in tension, compression, and bending. Electrical laboratories were equipped with generators, motors, transformers, switchboards, batteries, galvanometers, and measuring instruments, carrying the smell of warm insulation and the hum of rotating machines and the crackle of switchgear. Physics and chemistry laboratories supported the foundational years with benches, glassware, balances, and reagents. Surveying instruments (theodolites, levels) were stored and issued for fieldwork. Note: while the existence and expansion of these laboratories is documented, the granular detail here (specific instruments, room arrangements, sensory texture) is reconstructed from standard period engineering-laboratory practice for worldbuilding purposes rather than from verified inventories of the Giza school.
