Industrial technology in Egypt between 1915 and 1935 was in an early, nationally significant phase of growth, centered on processing the country's agricultural output, above all cotton. The dominant industries were cotton ginning and pressing, textile spinning and weaving, sugar refining, food processing, cement, and assorted light manufacturing. A landmark of the period was the founding of Bank Misr in 1920 by the nationalist economist Talaat Harb, and its flagship Misr Spinning and Weaving Company established in 1927 at El-Mahalla El-Kubra, which became Egypt's largest industrial facility and a symbol of economic nationalism aimed at manufacturing Egyptian cotton domestically rather than exporting it raw. The Misr group also launched ventures such as Misr Air (1932), reflecting diversification.

Industrial machinery was overwhelmingly imported from Europe and driven by steam engines, steam turbines, diesel engines, and electric motors, with factory power often distributed by belts and line shafts. Textile mills used mechanical spinning frames and power looms; ginning factories used mechanical gins; sugar mills used crushing and boiling plant. The workforce was largely recruited from the peasantry and trained on the job, and the company even sent technical-school graduates to Europe for training. Crucially, the technologies of modern industry did NOT exist in this period: there was no industrial automation or programmable control, no robotics, no electronics-based process control, no petrochemical or large-scale chemical industry of note, and limited heavy industry (no significant domestic steelmaking; the Helwan iron and steel works came only in the 1950s). Egyptian industry of 1915-1935 was mechanized but labor-intensive, based on steam, diesel, and early electric power.
