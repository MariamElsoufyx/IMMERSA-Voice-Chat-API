Graduation requirements at the engineering school during 1910-1940 centered on completing the full multi-year course of study and passing the required examinations at each stage, culminating in the award of an engineering diploma or degree. In the 1910s and early 1920s this typically meant completing roughly four years of study, with foundational common subjects followed by two years of departmental specialization, and demonstrating competence in both theoretical examinations and the practical, drawing, laboratory, and workshop components of the program. Following the reforms under Charles Andreae, the course was lengthened to five years from 1928-1930, restoring the preparatory year, so that graduation required successful completion of the preparatory year plus the subsequent years of specialized study.

A defining feature of the period was the close link between graduation and government employment: the majority of graduates were appointed to government engineering posts, especially in the Ministry of Public Works and irrigation services, and the school's purpose was explicitly to supply the ministries with technical personnel. The practical orientation of training meant graduates were expected to be capable of useful engineering work immediately. The classification of the final result (degree of "appreciation") influenced career placement. After the 1935 incorporation into the Egyptian University as the Faculty of Engineering, graduation conferred a recognized university-level engineering qualification. The first postgraduate (higher) studies in the Faculty did not begin until 1942, so throughout 1910-1940 the terminal qualification for nearly all students was the undergraduate engineering diploma/degree.
