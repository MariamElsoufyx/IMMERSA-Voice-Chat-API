Classroom layouts at the engineering school in early-twentieth-century Cairo followed the European lecture-hall and drawing-studio model of the period. Lecture rooms for theoretical subjects (mathematics, mechanics, hydraulics, physics) were typically tiered or stepped, with fixed wooden bench-desks rising in rows so that students could see the lecturer and the large blackboards at the front. The lecturer worked from a raised dais or platform, using chalk on extensive wall-mounted or sliding blackboards, often pulling down printed wall charts, diagrams, and rolled technical drawings to illustrate principles. Rooms had tall windows for daylight, high ceilings for ventilation, and were furnished plainly with hard surfaces that echoed sound.

Drawing and design studios, central to engineering and architecture training, were different in character: large, well-lit rooms filled with rows of individual drawing tables or sloped drafting boards, each equipped for a student's T-squares, set squares, and instruments, with north-facing or clerestory light prized for even illumination. Students spent long hours at these boards producing ink and pencil drawings. Specialized teaching rooms adjoined the laboratories and workshops so that theory could be paired immediately with demonstration and practice, reflecting the British-influenced emphasis on combining instruction with hands-on work. Class sizes were modest by later standards, as enrollment was limited and selective. Note: this layout description is reconstructed from the standard configuration of early-twentieth-century European and colonial engineering schools and the documented theory-plus-practice curriculum, rather than from surviving room-by-room plans of the Giza school, and is intended for historical worldbuilding and simulation.
