The curriculum structure evolved notably during 1910-1940. Historically the school had used a five-year program with a preparatory first year and specialization in the final two years, but the preparatory year had been cancelled in 1892. During the 1910s and early 1920s the course of study ran approximately four years, with common foundational subjects in the early years followed by departmental specialization in the final two years, as confirmed by the 1908 and 1916 reorganizations that placed specialization in the last two years.

A decisive restructuring came under director Charles Andreae (appointed 1928). The syllabi were upgraded and the course of study was lengthened from four years to five years, restoring the preparatory year, which returned in 1930 after a long absence. Under this model the first (preparatory) year delivered a broad common core in mathematics, physics, chemistry, mechanics, and technical drawing, irrespective of intended specialization. Subsequent years progressively introduced departmental subjects, with the heaviest specialization concentrated in the final years of study. Laboratory and workshop hours were integrated alongside lectures and exercise/problem sessions throughout the program. This five-year, preparatory-plus-specialization structure established in the 1928-1935 reforms became the durable template for Egyptian engineering education and persisted, in modified form, well beyond 1940. The curriculum's balance between theoretical foundations and practical training was a recurring point of tension, shifting with the changing French, British, and continental European influences on the school.
